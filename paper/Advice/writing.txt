<h2>Guidelines for Writing Papers</h2>
</div>

<p><i> This page is meant to assemble some information and guidelines
that students may consult about writing papers.  It is not anywhere
near complete yet.  Comments are welcome.</i></p>

<hr size="3"/>

<p> You will be called upon to write "papers" for much of your
professional life.  The "papers" - using the term broadly - may be as
diverse as term papers, qualifier papers, theses, research papers,
surveys, white papers, business plans, and design outlines.  The
remarks below are mostly geared toward the first several - more
academic - categories, but some of the remarks would apply to any
professional writing that you do.</p>

<p> Writing a paper should not be viewed as a chore, but as an
opportunity to organize your thoughts.  Often, organizing your
thoughts - even if about well-known topics - is a good service.  Even
if no one else benefits from this exercise, you will.  You will often
hear people say that they did good research on some topic, but could
never get around to writing it up.  They may be sincere, but they
would often be mistaken.  In most cases, the fact that they didn't
write it up means that they never quite completed the work.  In
writing it up, they would probably have realized what holes there were
in their arguments - the experiments they didn't run or the theorems
they didn't formulate and prove.</p>

<p>The importance of writing in good research is one of the reasons
for the CSC graduate faculty's decision to change the format of the
Ph.D. qualifier exams (aka written prelims).  Official information on
the CSC Ph.D. exams is available <a
href="http://www.csc.ncsu.edu/academics/graduate/degrees/phd.php#6">here</a>.</p>


<h4>Originality of Presentation</h4>

<p>One of the main claims that you make about anything you write is that
it is <i>original</i>.  Even if the paper is a description of other
people's work, the paper itself should be an original creation.  If
you include material written by others, it should be identified in
some way and credited to the source.  Not doing so may be considered
<i>plagiarism</i> (Latin for kidnapping), which is one of worst
charges to be levied against your work.  A lack of knowledge of
English is not a sufficiently good reason for plagiarism.</p>

<p> Quoting others verbatim is usually not a good idea anyway.  First,
what you say or write is for a purpose, and your purpose in your paper
is very likely different from the original authors' purpose in their
paper.  Therefore, even when you present their ideas, you have to
present them in a way that fits with what you are talking about now.
Second, mixing different styles of writing and interrupting your flow
with lots of quotations does not help your reader.</p>

<p> Indeed, there is usually little reason to quote others verbatim,
unless they have an especially pithy saying that you must include or
they made some especially specious claim that you must present as is
before you tear it apart.  For the above reasons, quotations must and
can usually be replaced by paraphrases (see below).</p>

<h4>Originality of Content</h4>

<p>Often, your paper should not only be original in its presentation,
but also in its content.  A research paper is meant to be original in
this sense.  For this reason, you should make sure that the main ideas
described in the paper - its main contributions - originated with you.
Even if you got the idea by yourself, you are expected to make a
reasonable effort to determine whether others had the same idea
before.</p>

<p> Of course, not every idea in a paper will be - or should be -
original.  You will almost always build on the work of others.
However, you should describe the work you build on.  If you do it in
your own words, it is called a <i>paraphrasing</i> and is generally
acceptable.  By the way, it is not acceptable to simply reorder some
bullet items, or make minor grammatical changes like converting a
sentence in the active voice to one in the passive voice.</p>

<p> Unless the work being described or paraphrased is well-known -
that is, part of the "folklore" - you should credit the people with
whom it originated.  In general, as topics become better known, you
should cite expository writings describing them, e.g., textbooks,
handbooks, encyclopedias, or survey articles.  For topics that are
relatively recent, it is better to credit the originators directly.</p>

<h4>Value Added</h4>

<p> Research writing is more than just a narrative of the steps you
took, but includes some assessment or evaluation of the ideas you
proposed and developed.</p>

<p>You should consider the value added by your paper - its contribution.
This could be an original scientific content (see above), or a novel
synthesis or analysis of existing ideas.  Often, the two go together.
If your paper's value added is in its original content, there would
usually be some problem that your approach can solve or some
phenomenon your theory can explain or predict.  If your paper's value
added is in a novel synthesis or analysis, then maybe you have
motivated a new way of looking at problems or existing solutions.</p>

<h4>Quality of Presentation</h4>

<p>There are a number of aspects to a good presentation.  These
especially include organization and the quality of the prose.  Several
of the resources listed below address these issues.  You should
consult some if not all of them.  Moreover, you should spell check and
proof read your papers.  In particular, when you read what you wrote
several days after you wrote it, you will often discover a number of
unclarities and ambiguities that weren't apparent earlier.</p>

<h4>Resources</h4>

<ul> 

<li><i><a href="http://www.acm.org/pubs/plagiarism%20policy.html">ACM
    Policy and Procedures on Plagiarism</a></i>: Make sure you respect
    it in all your work, even if not targeted at ACM venues.</li>

<li>Lyn Dupre, <i>Bugs in Writing : A Guide to Debugging Your
    Prose</i>, revised edition.  Addison-Wesley, 1998.  Includes a
    discussion on breaking the writer's block along with a lot of
    grammatical advice.</li>

<li>Nicholas J. Higham, <i>Handbook of Writing for the Mathematical
    Sciences</i>.  Second edition, 1998.  Society for Industrial &amp;
    Applied Mathematics; ISBN: 0898714206.  (First edition recommended
    by Dr. Dwyer and Dr. Funderlic.)</li>

<li>Bruce Ross-Larson, <i>Edit Yourself: A Manual for Everyone Who
    Works With Words</i>.  W.W. Norton &amp; Company, 1996; ISBN:
    0393313263.  (Recommended by Dr. Dwyer.)</li>

<li>William Strunk, Jr. and E.B. White, <i>Elements of Style</i>,
    Third edition, 1995.  Allyn &amp; Bacon; ISBN: 0205191584.  The first
    edition of this book is available online <a
    href="http://www.bartleby.com/141/">here</a> (Recommended by
    Dr. Rossie.)</li>

<li><a
    href="http://www.cs.cmu.edu/afs/cs.cmu.edu/user/mleone/web/how-to.html">Advice
    on Research and Writing</a>.</li>

</ul>
